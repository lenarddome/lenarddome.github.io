I am a computational cognitive scientist at the University of Tübingen. My research develops and evaluates process models of learning, generalisation, and decision-making, with a central commitment to model adequacy: whether models capture the right mechanisms, not merely the right outcomes. Much of my work approaches this through when intelligent systems fail — irrational generalisation across supervised and unsupervised task structures, and the computational principles that persist across radically different learning conditions (<a href="https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2608.06149">10.48550/arXiv.2608.06149</a>). Failures constrain the space of admissible mechanisms for biological intelligence far more sharply than successes do, since many mechanisms produce correct behaviour but only a few produce the right error under the right conditions. I am increasingly interested in extending this to any learning system, where the same logic applies: recent work of mine locates an instability in attention-learning architectures (used for theorising about human learning) and develops a general architectural change that fixes it — a result about a class of neural network architectures, derived rather than observed (<a href="https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2609.08615">10.48550/arXiv.2609.08615</a>). Analysing learning dynamics in tractable models this way seems to me the most promising route to formal accounts that hold for learning in minds and machines alike.
